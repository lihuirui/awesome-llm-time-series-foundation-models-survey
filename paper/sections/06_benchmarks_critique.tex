\section{Evaluations, Benchmarks, and Critical Perspectives}
\label{sec:eval}

The transition toward foundation models has fundamentally reshaped evaluation methodologies. However, it has also sparked intense debates regarding data contamination, inductive biases, and whether massive language backbones offer genuine advantages over specialized native architectures.

\subsection{Standardized Foundation Benchmarks}

Traditional time series evaluation relied on a small collection of homogeneous academic datasets (e.g., ETT, Weather, Electricity, Traffic). These benchmarks are inadequate for assessing foundation models due to narrow domain diversity and severe risks of test set memorization.

To establish rigorous evaluation standards, recent initiatives introduce expansive benchmark suites:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{GIFT-Eval}~\cite{salesforce2024gifteval}: Introduced by Salesforce Research, GIFT-Eval comprises over $143{,}000$ distinct time series spanning 7 diverse domains, 10 sampling frequencies, and both univariate and multivariate regimes. It establishes standardized metrics (CRPS, MASE, WAPE) across zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuned settings.
    \item \emph{fev-bench}~\cite{shchur2025fevbench}: Introduced by Shchur et al. (AWS and AutoGluon), fev-bench formulates a realistic benchmark encompassing 100 forecasting tasks across 7 domains. Crucially, 46 of these tasks incorporate dynamic covariates (e.g., promotional flags, weather drivers, calendar events) evaluated via the open-source \texttt{fev} library using bootstrapped confidence intervals and win-rate skill scores, directly addressing the historic deficiency where foundation models underperformed simple baselines by omitting exogenous features.
    \item \emph{It's TIME}~\cite{qiao2026time}: Qiao et al. (joint work between Tsinghua THUML and Ming Jin's group) establish next-generation benchmarks addressing multi-granularity task evaluation and rigorous train-test contamination audits, establishing standardized leakage-free splits across diverse operational horizons.
    \item \emph{Beyond Numerical Time Series}~\cite{chen2026beyondnumerical}: Chen et al. establish a unified multimodal evaluation framework pairing continuous quantitative sensors with heterogeneous textual descriptions, news contexts, and structural calendar metadata.
    \item \emph{AION}~\cite{zhan2026aion}: Zhan, Jin et al. formulate next-generation benchmarks moving beyond static univariate forecasting to evaluate agentic tool-use, multi-step structured reasoning, and contextual decision support over temporal observations.
    \item \emph{TimesX}~\cite{liu2026timesx}: Published at ICML 2026 by researchers from Georgia Tech and Google, TimesX provides a standardized benchmark for context-enriched multimodal forecasting, pairing high-frequency sensors with text narratives, calendar events, and exogenous market drivers across diverse global domains.
    \item \emph{TimeSeriesExam}~\cite{cai2024timeseriesexam}: Developed by the CMU MOMENT team, TimeSeriesExam formulates a diagnostic capability examination for foundation models. Rather than evaluating generic mean-squared errors on arbitrary test splits, it probes specific foundational abilities: trend extrapolation, seasonality decomposition, phase-shift invariance, and anomaly localization.
    \item \emph{LiveHouse-TS}~\cite{wen2026livehouse}: An open-world living benchmark platform that streams fresh real-world telemetry in real time, preventing static benchmark memorization and providing continuous evaluation under non-stationary concept drift.
    \item \emph{SciTS}~\cite{wu2025scits}: Scientific time series understanding and generation benchmark. Unlike commercial finance or traffic series, physical systems (spectroscopy, seismology, fluid dynamics, astrophysics) exhibit strict conservation laws and differential constraints. SciTS evaluates whether foundation models comprehend underlying physical laws rather than merely extrapolating statistical autocorrelations.
    \item \emph{Insight Miner}~\cite{zhang2025insightminer}: A large-scale cross-domain dataset designed for time series alignment with natural language. Insight Miner pairs complex multivariate temporal trajectories with human-curated analytical insights, causal event narratives, and question-answer pairs across finance, healthcare, and industrial telemetry.
    \item \emph{Time-MMD}~\cite{yue2024timemmd}: A multi-domain multimodal dataset evaluating the integration of numerical time series with unstructured text reports, news articles, and domain metadata across 9 industries.
    \item \emph{Monash Time Series Repository}: The classical zero-shot transfer benchmark comprising dozens of public collections across economics, energy, and meteorology, widely adopted to evaluate out-of-distribution generalization.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Critical Perspectives: Are LLMs Actually Useful for Time Series?}

A pivotal empirical critique emerged with the investigation by Fons et al.~\cite{fons2024arellmsuseful}, entitled \emph{``Are Language Models Actually Useful for Time Series Forecasting?''}
Through extensive ablation experiments across multiple LLM-based forecasters (including GPT4TS~\cite{zhou2023onefitsall} and Time-LLM~\cite{jin2023timellm}), the authors isolated the impact of pre-trained language weights by comparing:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Frozen pre-trained language weights (e.g., GPT-2, Llama-2);
    \item Randomly initialized transformer weights with the same architecture;
    \item Compact specialized baselines (such as PatchTST~\cite{nie2023patchtst} and lightweight MLPs).
\end{enumerate}

Their empirical findings revealed striking conclusions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Replacing pre-trained language weights with \emph{randomly initialized weights} resulted in comparable, and in several cases superior, forecasting accuracy.
    \item Much of the empirical gains reported in early LLM4TS literature originated from the structural benefits of \emph{subseries patching} and normalization, rather than any semantic or temporal reasoning inherent in linguistic pre-training.
    \item In terms of operational efficiency, frozen $7\text{B}$ parameter language models require up to $100\times$ more memory and $50\times$ longer inference latency than compact native models like TTM~\cite{ekambaram2024ttm} ($8\text{M}$ parameters) or Timer~\cite{liu2024timer} ($84\text{M}$ parameters), which consistently achieve competitive or superior accuracy.
\end{itemize}

These findings indicate that while LLMs are uniquely valuable for multimodal tasks (text-grounded reasoning, interactive analysis, and report generation), deploying frozen multi-billion parameter text backbones solely for univariate numeric forecasting represents an inefficient architectural detour.

Subsequent studies explore the emergent role of foundation models as evaluators and agents:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{TimeVista}~\cite{chen2026timevista}: Chen et al. (THUML) observe that standard point metrics (MAE, MSE) penalize benign phase shifts drastically while ignoring visual shape plausibility, turning-point fidelity, and regime continuity. By deploying Vision-Language Models as judges, TimeVista demonstrates that VLM-based perceptual evaluation achieves an $84\%$ rank correlation with human domain specialists, establishing a perceptual evaluation framework beyond scalar error metrics.
    \item \emph{Forecast Workflow Bench}~\cite{nagashima2026forecastworkflow}: Nagashima investigates whether LLM agents can orchestrate time-series foundation models under realistic operational tool budgets. Evaluating across multi-step operational forecasting workflows, the benchmark reveals that effective agentic decisions require balancing statistical forecast accuracy against inference latency and API cost budgets.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data Contamination, Temporal Leakage, and Probabilistic Reliability}

\subsubsection{Benchmark Contamination Audits}
A critical vulnerability in contemporary foundation model evaluation is \emph{benchmark contamination}. In natural language processing, models frequently memorize test sets crawled from public websites; in time series, foundation models trained on open-source archives (e.g., LOTSA, Monash, GitHub telemetry) inadvertently ingest the training and testing portions of benchmark datasets (e.g., ETT, Weather)~\cite{gong2025rethinkingeval}.

Gong et al.~\cite{gong2025rethinkingeval} systematically audited 12 major foundation models and discovered widespread temporal overlap between pretraining datasets and standard test benchmarks. When evaluated on genuinely unseen, closed-source industrial telemetry collected post-2025, zero-shot performance degraded by $18\%$ to $42\%$ across all major models. This underscores the urgent need for living, dynamic benchmarks (such as LiveHouse-TS~\cite{wen2026livehouse}) where evaluation datasets are continuously refreshed.

\subsubsection{Probabilistic Reliability and Quantile Calibration}
Beyond mean error metrics, real-world deployment in safety-critical sectors (energy grids, algorithmic trading, intensive care) demands calibrated uncertainty intervals. In a recent rigorous audit, Li et al.~\cite{li2026evaluatingreliability} investigated the probabilistic reliability of zero-shot TSFMs. Their findings reveal a concerning dichotomy: models achieving competitive point forecast errors often display severe quantile miscalibration. Specifically, $90\%$ prediction intervals frequently attain actual coverage below $70\%$ (under-coverage) on non-stationary out-of-distribution series, or suffer from quantile crossing artifacts where estimated quantiles violate monotonicity. This reinforces the necessity of reporting both CRPS and interval coverage scores alongside standard MAE and MSE metrics.

\subsection{Empirical Scaling Laws and Meta-Regression in Temporal Data}
\label{sec:scaling_laws}

Do foundation models for time series exhibit the predictable power-law scaling observed in NLP?
Recent empirical studies on large pre-trained models—notably Time-MoE~\cite{shi2024timemoe} ($2.4\text{B}$ parameters, $300\text{B}$ points), Sundial~\cite{thuml2025sundial} ($1.5\text{B}$ parameters), Timer-S1~\cite{thuml2026timers1} ($8.3\text{B}$ parameters), and Toto 2.0~\cite{datadog2026toto2} ($2.5\text{B}$ parameters)—demonstrate that when data quality and volume are rigorously maintained, validation loss follows a bivariate power law as a joint function of non-embedding parameter count $N$ and unique training tokens $D$:
\begin{equation}
    L(N, D) = \left( \frac{N_c}{N} \right)^{\alpha_N} + \left( \frac{D_c}{D} \right)^{\alpha_D} + L_0,
\end{equation}
where $N_c, D_c$ denote characteristic scale constants and $L_0$ represents the irreducible entropy of stochastic observation noise. Given the training compute budget $C \approx 6 N D$, the optimal parameter allocation follows $N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{\frac{\alpha_D}{\alpha_N + \alpha_D}}$ and $D_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{\frac{\alpha_N}{\alpha_N + \alpha_D}}$.

Meta-regression over reported validation curves across foundational architectures reveals crucial empirical characteristics:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{Fitted Exponents}: Time-MoE~\cite{shi2024timemoe} reports $\alpha_N \approx 0.089$ and $\alpha_D \approx 0.112$ across models ranging from $50\text{M}$ to $2.4\text{B}$ parameters on Time-300B; Sundial~\cite{thuml2025sundial} reports $\alpha_N \approx 0.076$ on UTSD-3; and Toto 2.0~\cite{datadog2026toto2} reports $\alpha_N \approx 0.081$ and $\alpha_D \approx 0.098$ under Maximal Update Parametrization ($\mu\text{P}$). In comparison, LLM language pretraining exhibits $\alpha_N \approx 0.076$--$0.088$ and $\alpha_D \approx 0.095$--$0.120$. This confirms that temporal transformers obey bona fide neural scaling laws.
    \item \emph{The Temporal Entropy Saturation Barrier}: In time series, $\alpha_N$ exhibits early saturation when scaling beyond $1\text{B}$ parameters if trained solely on public sensor repositories. Because real-world telemetry frequently contains repetitive stationary waveforms with low Kolmogorov complexity, models quickly exhaust novel temporal structures. To sustain power-law scaling ($\alpha_N > 0$), state-of-the-art pipelines (Chronos, Time-MoE, Tabby~\cite{xie2026tabby}) inject synthetic generative processes (mixtures of non-linear differential equations and Gaussian process kernels), proving that synthetic diversity is mandatory to maintain compute-optimal scaling in time series.
\end{itemize}

Synthesis of computational profiling across the literature yields three decisive insights:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{The Autoregressive Generation Bottleneck}: Models relying on scalar or token-wise autoregression (e.g., original Chronos~\cite{ansari2024chronos} iterating over 4096 bins) incur $H$ sequential attention forward passes to forecast an $H$-step horizon. In production benchmarks, Chronos-Bolt resolves this bottleneck by shifting from point-wise autoregression to patch-level direct quantile regression, achieving a documented $250\times$ inference speedup and $20\times$ memory reduction while matching or exceeding zero-shot accuracy.
    \item \emph{The Extreme Throughput Gap (Mixers vs. Reprogrammed LLMs)}: Lightweight architectural designs—exemplified by IBM's Tiny Time Mixers (TTM~\cite{ekambaram2024ttm}, 1M--8M parameters) and LightGTS~\cite{wang2025lightgts} (5.8M parameters)—execute full batch inference in under $5\text{ms}$ on commodity hardware with zero KV-cache memory overhead. In contrast, reprogrammed LLMs (Time-LLM~\cite{jin2023timellm}, AutoTimes~\cite{liu2024autotimes}) require multi-second latency per inference batch and allocate over $14\text{GB}$ of GPU VRAM. For high-frequency industrial automation where millisecond response times are mandatory, compact native mixers represent the only viable foundation paradigm.
    \item \emph{In-Context Bayesian Inference vs. Score-Based Diffusion}: Prior-Data Fitted Networks (TabPFN-TS~\cite{muller2025tabpfnts}) offer unique operational trade-offs: while context-sequence attention scales quadratically with history length, they execute zero gradient updates, eliminating all fine-tuning optimizer overhead. Conversely, continuous score-based flow matching (FlowState~\cite{king2025flowstate}, UrbanDiT~\cite{li2024urbandit}) demands multiple numerical ODE integration steps ($K \in [10, 50]$), exchanging inference latency for mathematically continuous, sampling-rate-equivariant trajectories.
\end{enumerate}

HoliBench uncovers that integer post-training quantization (PTQ) reduces latency only on architectures equipped with specialized low-precision compute pipelines (e.g., INT8 Tensor Cores or NEON vector units); on general-purpose microcontrollers lacking integer SIMD, quantization merely reduces memory footprint while potentially increasing execution latency due to dequantization overhead. Furthermore, HoliBench demonstrates that standalone single-model profiles compose with high fidelity ($1.2\%$ latency and $2.5\%$ power error) under co-resident execution, enabling optimal multi-model pipeline configuration without combinatorial profiling.
    \item \emph{Resource-Aware Mixed-Precision FPGA Quantization}: Ling et al.~\cite{ling2024resource} resolve the hardware-level bottleneck of deploying time-series Transformers onto resource-constrained embedded FPGAs (Xilinx Spartan-7 XC7S15). By formulating a resource-aware mixed-precision quantization methodology (allocating INT8 to sensitive cross-attention maps and INT4 to feed-forward blocks) and optimizing block RAM (BRAM) storage templates, they achieve hardware resource estimation within $3\%$ of physical silicon synthesis, unlocking deployability for previously non-synthesizable transformer configurations.
    \item \emph{Carbon-Aware Dynamic Dispatch (FM-CAC)}: Yang et al.~\cite{yang2026fmcac} formulate FM-CAC, establishing a proactive carbon-aware control framework for battery-buffered edge AI systems. By deploying edge-friendly zero-shot TSFMs to forecast temporal marginal emissions and grid carbon intensity, FM-CAC feeds predictions into a dynamic programming solver with deferred cost attribution. This decouples energy harvesting from compute execution, reducing carbon emissions by up to $65.6\%$ while maintaining near-maximum inference accuracy across continuous edge vision and time-series pipelines.
\end{enumerate}

As detailed in Table~\ref{tab:energy_quantization_benchmarks}, lightweight architectures (e.g., Cheraghinia MLP~\cite{cheraghinia2025lightweight}, TTM~\cite{ekambaram2024ttm}, and APEX-Edge~\cite{pradhan2026apex}) consume between $0.18\text{ mJ}$ and $28.5\text{ mJ}$ per inference, operating comfortably within battery-buffered and energy-harvesting IoT regimes with negligible ($<1.2\%$) degradation under INT8 quantization. In contrast, multi-billion parameter LLMs demand over $18\text{ Joules}$ per forward pass and over $14\text{ GB}$ of VRAM, underscoring that compact, quantization-resilient native models are mandatory for sustainable edge intelligence.

\subsection{Data Contamination, Pretraining Familiarity Bias, and Automated Continuous Auditing}
\label{sec:data_contamination_auditing}

The rapid proliferation of time-series foundation models has exposed an acute methodological challenge: \emph{benchmark data contamination and temporal leakage}. Unlike natural language corpora where test sets can be filtered via string matching, time series consist of continuous, normalized numerical sequences that undergo varying degrees of preprocessing (standardization, differencing, patch discretization). Furthermore, because the pretraining datasets of many prominent models (e.g., UTSD, Chronos-Data, Monash) aggregate open-source archives, classic benchmarks (ETT, Weather, Electricity, Traffic) are frequently ingested during pretraining, rendering reported zero-shot performance suspect.

Recent literature systematically tackles this vulnerability through automated auditing frameworks, leakage-free simulation gyms, and temporal familiarity metrics:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{TSFMAudit: Automated Temporal Contamination Scanning (Li et al., 2026)}~\cite{li2026tsfmaudit}: Li et al. develop TSFMAudit, establishing the first formal auditing suite for detecting pretraining contamination in time-series foundation models. TSFMAudit introduces two complementary statistical tests: (1) \emph{Quantile-Bin N-Gram Hashing}, which maps continuous floating-point sequences into discrete symbolic tokens to detect verbatim sequence memorization, and (2) \emph{Mutual Information Permutation Testing}, which quantifies non-linear statistical dependence between candidate pretraining segments and benchmark test sets. Auditing multiple prominent open-weight foundation models, TSFMAudit reveals that up to $18.4\%$ of standard benchmark sequences exhibit statistically significant memorization signatures, leading to artificially deflated zero-shot test errors.
    \item \emph{Pretraining Familiarity Bias Survives Temporal Hold-Outs (Moghadasi \& Ghaderi, 2026)}~\cite{moghadasi2026familiarity}: Moghadasi and Ghaderi challenge the pervasive research convention that temporal hold-outs (e.g., training on pre-2020 data and testing on post-2020 data) guarantee an uncontaminated evaluation. They show that because physical data-generating processes (e.g., weather cycles, power grid load curves) exhibit stationary dynamical attractors, a foundation model exposed to historical records retains profound \emph{pretraining familiarity}. When evaluated on temporally separated hold-outs from familiar domains versus genuine cross-domain datasets, foundation models experience performance gaps of up to $42\%$, proving that reported zero-shot scores frequently conflate cross-domain generalization with domain-specific familiarity.
    \item \emph{Empirical Stress-Testing on Volatile Infrastructure (Pan \& Ezzat, 2026)}~\cite{pan2026evaluating}: Pan and Ezzat conduct an exhaustive empirical audit of zero-shot foundation models (Chronos-Bolt, TimesFM, Moirai) on electricity price markets (PJM, EPEX, and Australian NEM). Characterized by extreme price spikes and renewable penetration, electricity markets violate Gaussian stationarity. Their audit uncovers that univariate foundation models degrade by $28\%$ compared to localized gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM) due to their inability to ingest critical exogenous covariates (temperature, transmission capacity). Furthermore, contamination risk is heightened because public market data is ubiquitously crawled into pretraining corpora without provenance tracking.
    \item \emph{Forecast-Dojo: Replayable Environments for LLM Forecasting Agents (Ye et al., 2026)}~\cite{ye2026forecastdojo}: Ye et al. introduce Forecast-Dojo, a leak-free replayable simulation gym that benchmarks multi-agent forecasting systems on resolved prediction market questions (from Polymarket and Metaculus). Forecast-Dojo deterministically pairs real-world questions with dated, timestamped global news archives, strictly eliminating future lookahead bias. In this controlled environment, agents must gather historical evidence, write Python code, and generate calibrated probabilistic forecasts, providing a rigorous testbed for evaluating genuine temporal reasoning without data contamination.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Distribution-Free Conformal Prediction and Statistical Coverage Guarantees}
\label{sec:conformal_prediction_guarantees}

A profound deficiency in contemporary time series foundation models is their vulnerability to \emph{uncalibrated probabilistic uncertainty}. While foundation models deploy various heads---such as categorical cross-entropy over quantized bins (Chronos), continuous Student's $t$ / Gaussian parameters (TimesFM, Moirai), or diffusion score matching---their nominal prediction intervals lack rigorous finite-sample coverage guarantees. Under out-of-distribution drift, non-stationary variance scaling, or heavy-tailed shocks, standard foundation model intervals suffer from catastrophic empirical undercoverage (e.g., nominal $90\%$ prediction intervals achieving only $65$--$75\%$ empirical coverage).

\emph{Conformal Prediction (CP)} provides an elegant, model-agnostic, distribution-free mathematical framework that converts any point or quantile forecaster into set-valued prediction regions $\mathcal{C}_{1-\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{n+1})$ guaranteeing exact finite-sample marginal coverage:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}_{1-\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{n+1})\right) \ge 1 - \alpha,
\end{equation}
for a user-specified significance level $\alpha \in (0, 1)$, without requiring Gaussianity, linearity, or asymptotic sample sizes.

Recent breakthroughs systematically integrate conformal inference with time series foundation models:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{Foundation Models for Time Series in Conformal Prediction (Achour et al., 2025)}~\cite{achour2025conformal}: Achour et al. uncover a structural synergy between zero-shot foundation models and \emph{Split Conformal Prediction (SCP)}. In classical machine learning, limited data must be bifurcated between parameter fitting $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ and nonconformity calibration $\mathcal{D}_{\text{calib}}$, severely impairing calibration sample size. Because zero-shot foundation models (Lag-Llama, TimesFM, Chronos) require zero target training data, $100\%$ of available in-domain historical data can be dedicated to calibration. Evaluating across ERCOT, NN5, and M3 benchmarks, Achour et al. show that zero-shot TSFM conformal intervals achieve strictly valid $1-\alpha$ coverage while producing significantly narrower interval widths than classical GBDT or ARIMA baselines, demonstrating that foundation backbones stabilize post-hoc conformal calibration.
    \item \emph{Adaptive Conformal Anomaly Detection for Signal Monitoring (Martinez Gil et al., 2026)}~\cite{martinezgil2026adaptive}: Martinez Gil et al. formulate adaptive conformal anomaly detection for industrial telemetry. Rather than applying arbitrary thresholding heuristics to foundation model reconstruction residuals, the authors utilize weighted quantile conformal prediction bounds to transform residuals into exact, false-alarm-rate controlled p-values. By adaptively updating nonconformity weights online as temporal distributions drift, this framework maintains a guaranteed false alarm rate ($\le \alpha$) on streaming IoT sensor workloads without retraining foundation backbones.
    \item \emph{RareCP: Regime-Aware Retrieval for Efficient Conformal Prediction (Heurich et al., 2026)}~\cite{heurich2026rarecp}: Standard conformal inference assumes exchangeability, an assumption systematically violated by temporal autocorrelation and regime-switching dynamics. Heurich, Granz, and Landgraf introduce RareCP, which decouples smooth distribution drift from distinct macroeconomic/operational regimes. RareCP deploys a Mixture of Experts (MoE) with cosine attention alongside a hypernetwork that retrieves the top-$k$ most relevant historical calibration contexts for any query sequence. By computing asymmetric weighted conformal quantiles over retrieved residuals on the GIFT-Eval benchmark, RareCP preserves nominal coverage guarantees while shrinking interval width by up to $22\%$ compared to Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) and raw foundation model quantiles.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Differentially Private Generative Twins and Provable Leakage-Free Benchmark Curation}
\label{sec:dp_generative_twins}

As foundation models scale to ingest petabytes of uncurated web and institutional telemetry, benchmark data contamination and sensitive training leakage emerge as primary obstacles to empirical evaluation. Evaluating models on real-world industrial, financial, and medical time series risks not only memorization bias, but also severe privacy violations through membership and attribute inference attacks. Constructing synthetic generative data twins with provable $(\epsilon, \delta)$-Differential Privacy (DP) guarantees provides a principled pathway toward guaranteed leak-free benchmark curation:

Formally, a randomized generative or forecasting algorithm $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $(\epsilon, \delta)$-Differential Privacy if, for all neighboring sequential datasets $\mathcal{D}, \mathcal{D}'$ differing by at most one individual's temporal trajectory, and for all measurable output subsets $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \text{Range}(\mathcal{M})$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}) \in \mathcal{S}) \le e^\epsilon \, \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}') \in \mathcal{S}) + \delta.
\end{equation}
In continuous and autoregressive time series, achieving meaningful privacy guarantees is notoriously difficult due to strong temporal autocorrelation: naive step-wise Gaussian noise injection destroys multi-step dynamical coherence, whereas trajectory-level clipping yields catastrophically loose privacy budgets $\epsilon$. Recent breakthroughs systematically bridge this tension:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{Privacy Amplification by Structured Subsampling for Time Series (Schuchardt et al., 2025)}~\cite{schuchardt2025privacy}: Accepted as an ICML 2025 Spotlight, Schuchardt et al. establish the first mathematically rigorous framework for training deep foundation forecasting models under Differential Privacy with structured temporal subsampling. Standard DP-SGD assumes independent uniform Poisson subsampling, which completely violates sequential temporal dependencies when dividing long series into sliding training windows. Schuchardt et al. develop a novel Rényi Differential Privacy (RDP) accountant specifically designed for structured overlapping subsampling schemes. By accounting for cross-window temporal correlations, their framework enables deep patch transformers to train with strictly amplified privacy budgets ($\epsilon < 2.0$), maintaining high forecasting utility across electricity, traffic, and clinical EHR datasets while mathematically proving that sensitive temporal history cannot be reconstructed by an adversary.
    \item \emph{TSGBench: Standardized Benchmarking of Generative Time Series (Ang et al., 2024)}~\cite{ang2024tsgbench}: In VLDB 2024, Ang et al. introduce TSGBench, the first unified empirical benchmark specifically assessing time series generation methods across fidelity, privacy preservation, and domain adaptation. TSGBench establishes the rigorous ``Train on Synthetic, Test on Real'' (TSTR) protocol alongside ``Train on Real, Test on Synthetic'' (TRTS), evaluating 15 generative paradigms across 12 diverse real-world datasets using 12 complementary metrics (including Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD), discriminative classifier error, predictive autocorrelation fidelity, and empirical distance-to-closest-record membership inference risk). TSGBench reveals that generative foundation models paired with privacy-preserving regularization produce synthetic data twins that match real-world forecasting utility while provably defending against downstream data extraction, paving the way for standardized leak-free benchmark distribution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Long-Context Memory Retention, Dynamic Graph Benchmarks, and Relational Conformal Inference}
\label{sec:memory_graphs_conformal}

As time-series foundation models scale across context horizons and complex relational topologies, evaluating empirical performance requires investigating the fundamental nature of \emph{memory retention} and \emph{graph-structured uncertainty guarantees}. In long-sequence forecasting, capturing multi-scale seasonal patterns and non-local regime transitions depends on how past temporal observations are stored and retrieved:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{t+1:t+H} = f_\theta(\mathbf{X}_{t-L+1:t}, \mathcal{M}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{M}$ represents the model's memory architecture. In standard self-attention transformers, $\mathcal{M}$ is an uncompressed external KV-cache $\mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$, providing exact associative recall at the cost of $O(L^2)$ computation and memory. In structured state-space models (Mamba/S4), $\mathcal{M}$ is compressed into a fixed-size internal recurrent state $\mathbf{h}_t \in \mathbb{R}^N$ with linear $O(L)$ training and $O(1)$ generation memory. However, whether internal state compression incurs catastrophic loss of long-term temporal information has remained a disputed empirical question. Furthermore, extending foundation models to dynamic graphs and microservices demands structure-aware benchmark suites and distribution-free relational uncertainty quantification:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{Memory Retention and the Spectrum of Deep Time-Series Architectures (Nguyen et al., 2026)}~\cite{nguyen2026memory}: Nguyen et al. formalize a unified taxonomy of memory mechanisms across deep time-series models, organizing architectures along a continuum from internal memory (fixed-size states in RNNs and SSMs) to external memory (transformer KV-caches, retrieval-augmented buffers, and agentic key-value stores). Through extensive empirical stress testing across extended lookback horizons ($L \in [10^2, 10^5]$), the authors uncover a fundamental architectural trade-off: while selective SSMs (Mamba) eliminate transformer memory explosion and maintain sub-linear latency scaling, their fixed-dimensional recurrent state exhibits gradual information decay on high-entropy multi-frequency signals. Conversely, full-attention transformers preserve distant dependencies without decay, but experience quadratic memory bottlenecks and latency degradation beyond $8\text{K}$ tokens. This systematic audit demonstrates that hybrid architectures interleaving selective state-space layers with sparse retrieval stores represent the optimal frontier for million-step context windows.
    \item \emph{ChronoGraph: Real-World Graph-Structured Microservice Telemetry (Lutu et al., 2025)}~\cite{lutu2025chronograph}: Presented at the NeurIPS 2025 Workshop on Foundation Models, Lutu et al. introduce ChronoGraph, a novel benchmark suite constructed from real-world production cloud microservices. Each node in ChronoGraph represents a distributed microservice emitting multivariate telemetry streams (CPU, memory, and network throughput), while directed graph edges encode real-time call dependencies. Uniquely, ChronoGraph provides expert-annotated incident outage windows, enabling rigorous evaluation of foundation model robustness during cascading system failures. Empirical evaluation demonstrates that standard flat multivariate foundation models fail to localize anomaly cascades, whereas graph-aware foundation architectures incorporating topological message passing reduce incident detection delay by up to $42\%$.
    \item \emph{STOIC: Relational and Sequential Conformal Prediction over Graphs (Niresi et al., 2026)}~\cite{niresi2026graphconformal}: Niresi et al. address the open challenge of providing distribution-free uncertainty quantification on complex graph-structured energy time series. In interconnected electricity grids and district heating networks, point forecasts generated by spatial-temporal graph neural networks (STGNNs) lack safety guarantees. Standard split conformal prediction fails because spatial graph correlations violate exchangeability assumptions. Niresi et al. propose STOIC (Spatial-Temporal Graph Conformal Prediction with In-Context Learning), which reformulates STGNN prediction residuals into tabular representations and leverages the zero-shot calibration power of tabular foundation models. Without task-specific retraining, STOIC calibrates prediction intervals that simultaneously account for temporal autocorrelation and graph relational dependencies, delivering strictly valid $1-\alpha$ marginal coverage with narrower interval widths than traditional conformal baselines across five real-world utility networks.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Transferability Estimation, Vintage Data Revisions, and Heterogeneous Batch Scheduling}
\label{sec:transferability_vintages_batch_scheduling}

As the foundation paradigm matures, critical systemic questions shift from ``can foundation models predict time series?'' to: (1) \emph{How can practitioners predict whether a foundation model will transfer positively or negatively to a new target domain without incurring the prohibitive compute cost of full fine-tuning?} (2) \emph{How do real-world revisions and publication lags contaminate historical benchmarks?} and (3) \emph{How should foundation models be scheduled across heterogeneous compute clusters serving multi-tenant closed-loop workloads?}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{TimeTic: In-Context Transferability Estimation (Yao et al., 2025)}~\cite{yao2025timetic}: While zero-shot performance is widely used as a heuristic proxy for selecting foundation models, Yao et al. demonstrate that zero-shot error correlates poorly with post-fine-tuning adaptation quality. Evaluating 10 foundation models across 10 diverse datasets, they introduce TimeTic. TimeTic reformulates transferability estimation as an in-context learning problem solved by tabular foundation models. By extracting statistical dataset meta-features paired with \emph{layer-wise entropy evolution vectors} (capturing how representation uncertainty shifts across transformer depths), TimeTic predicts the downstream ranking of foundation models before fine-tuning. TimeTic achieves a mean Spearman rank correlation of $\approx 0.60$ with ground-truth fine-tuned accuracy—representing a $30\%$ improvement over zero-shot ranking proxies—effectively preventing costly fine-tuning on ill-suited architectures.

    \item \emph{Diversified Scaling Inference: Expanding Generative Support (Hua et al., 2026)}~\cite{hua2026diversified}: Hua et al. investigate whether computational scaling laws can be unlocked strictly at inference time without modifying model parameters. In standard autoregressive generation, foundation models frequently collapse into conservative mode seeking, under-exploring low-probability but high-impact future branches. Hua et al. propose \emph{Diversified Scaling Inference}, injecting tailored signal perturbations during decoding to broaden generative support. They theoretically establish a \emph{critical sample threshold} beyond which diversified sampling provably outperforms standard temperature sampling. Furthermore, they define the \emph{RobustMSE} metric to quantify the latent headroom performance of frozen TSFMs under fixed inference budgets, proving that controlled diversity scaling yields up to $14.8\%$ error reductions across Chronos, TimesFM, and MOIRAI.

    \item \emph{VINTAGE-TS: Auditing Hindsight Bias in Revised Time Series (Ahmad, 2026)}~\cite{ahmad2026vintagets}: A pervasive but largely unaddressed contamination vector in macroeconomic, climate, and epidemiological forecasting is \emph{data revision}. Statistical agencies routinely revise historical time series months or years after the fact as additional censuses arrive. Foundation models pretrained on contemporary retrospective downloads inadvertently train on ``mature'' data that was fundamentally unavailable to human decision-makers at prediction time, creating artificial performance inflations. Ahmad formulates VINTAGE-TS, building upon a frozen Chronos-2 backbone. VINTAGE-TS introduces dual temporal indexing: distinguishing observation time $t$ from information-availability time $\tau \ge t$. Evaluated on the ALFRED real-time vintage database, VINTAGE-TS isolates the severe performance degradation ($18\%\text{--}35\%$ higher RMSE) that occurs when models forecast unrevised initial releases versus revised mature series, providing a reproducible benchmarking harness for revision-consistent foundation modeling.

    \item \emph{Armory: Lookahead Batch Scheduling for Robot Policy Serving (Bansal et al., 2026)}~\cite{bansal2026armory}: Transitioning foundation models to physical embodiment, Bansal et al. address the challenge of serving large policy models across heterogeneous multi-robot fleets. In closed-loop robotic control, foundation models generate ``action chunks'' (sequences of future motor actions) executed over receding horizons. Because different robots consume actions at varying speeds and experience disparate network latencies, standard naive batching induces either action queue starvation (causing robot stalling) or excessive latency. Bansal et al. propose Armory, formulating action chunk scheduling as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). Armory deploys a Lookahead scheduler that simulates candidate batch execution over a server-side mirror of each robot's queue state, maximizing latency-normalized execution throughput by up to $18\%$ over standard FIFO and priority schedulers.
\end{itemize}
